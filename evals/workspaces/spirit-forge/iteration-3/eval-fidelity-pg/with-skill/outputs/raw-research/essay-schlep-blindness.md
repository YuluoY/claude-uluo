# Schlep Blindness

By Paul Graham, January 2012

There are great startup ideas lying around unexploited right under our noses. One reason we don't see them is a phenomenon I call schlep blindness. "Schlep" comes from Yiddish and refers to tedious, unpleasant tasks.

## Hackers and Schleps

No one likes schleps, but hackers especially dislike them. Most young startup founders wish they could just write clever code, put it on a server, and watch money come in — without ever having to talk to a user, negotiate with other companies, or deal with other people's broken code. I have never seen this work.

At Y Combinator, one of our functions is to teach founders about the inevitability of schleps. I learned this myself in 1995, when I was still trying to convince myself I could start a company by just writing code. The fact is, schleps are not merely inevitable but pretty much what business consists of. A company is defined by the schleps it will undertake.

Schleps should be dealt with the same way you'd dive into a cold pool: just do it. I'm not saying you should seek out unpleasant work — only that you should never shrink from it if it's on the path to something great.

## Unconscious Avoidance

The most dangerous thing about schlep blindness is that much of it is unconscious. Your unconscious won't even let you see ideas that involve painful schleps.

Most people don't consciously decide not to be Olympic-level athletes. Their unconscious mind decides for them, shrinking from the work involved.

## Stripe: The Perfect Example

The most striking example of schlep blindness I know is Stripe. For more than a decade, every hacker who'd ever had to process payments online knew how painful the experience was. Thousands of people understood this problem. Yet when starting startups, they chose to build recipe sites or local event aggregators — things few people wanted — instead of fixing one of the most important components of the world's infrastructure.

Because schlep blindness prevented them from even considering the idea. Probably no one who applied to Y Combinator to work on a recipe site began by asking: "Should we fix payments, or build a recipe site?" They never saw the payments idea, because their unconscious mind shrank from the complications: bank deals, fraud prevention, keeping servers secure, regulations. It's a lot more intimidating to start Stripe than a recipe site.

## Why Ambitious Ideas Win

That scariness makes ambitious ideas doubly valuable. Beyond their intrinsic value, they're like undervalued stocks — there's less demand for them among founders. If you choose an ambitious idea, you'll have less competition, because everyone else will have been frightened off by the challenges involved.

## Overcoming Schlep Blindness

The most valuable antidote to schlep blindness is probably ignorance. Most successful founders, if they'd known what they were getting into, might never have started. This may explain why the most successful startups so often have young founders.

Founders grow with their problems, but no one seems able to foresee this growth — not even older, more experienced founders. Younger founders benefit from two mistakes that cancel out: they don't know how much they can grow, but they also don't know how much they'll need to. Older founders make only the first mistake.

But ignorance can't solve everything. Some ideas involve glaringly scary schleps that anyone can see. For those, try a mental trick: take yourself out of the picture. Instead of asking "What problem should I solve?" ask "What problem do I wish someone else would solve for me?" Anyone who processed payments before Stripe would have wished for Stripe.

It's too late now to be Stripe, but there's still plenty broken in the world, if you know how to see it.
