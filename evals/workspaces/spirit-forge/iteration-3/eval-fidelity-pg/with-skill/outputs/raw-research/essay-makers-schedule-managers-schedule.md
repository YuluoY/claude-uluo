# Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule

By Paul Graham, July 2009

One reason programmers dislike meetings so much is that they're on a different type of schedule from other people. Meetings cost them more.

## Two Types of Schedule

**Manager's schedule:** The traditional appointment book, with each day cut into one-hour intervals. You can block off several hours for a task, but by default you change what you're doing every hour. Scheduling a meeting on this schedule is merely a practical problem — find an open slot.

**Maker's schedule:** Used by programmers, writers, and anyone who makes things. They generally prefer to use time in units of half a day at least. You can't write or program well in units of an hour. That's barely enough time to get started.

## Why Meetings Destroy Maker Productivity

A single meeting can blow a whole afternoon by breaking it into two pieces, each too small to do anything hard in. Plus you have to remember you have a meeting. For someone on the manager's schedule, that's fine — there's always something coming next. For a maker, having a meeting is like throwing an exception. It doesn't just cause a task switch; it changes the mode in which you work.

One meeting can sometimes affect a whole day. If I know the afternoon is going to be broken up, I'm slightly less likely to start something ambitious in the morning. This sounds oversensitive, but ask yourself: don't your spirits rise at the prospect of having an entire day free to work? Well, that means your spirits are correspondingly depressed when you don't.

## When Schedules Collide

Each schedule works fine by itself. Problems arise when they meet. Since most powerful people are on the manager's schedule, they're in a position to make everyone resonate at their frequency.

## How Y Combinator Handles It

Nearly all investors, including all VCs I know, operate on the manager's schedule. But Y Combinator runs on the maker's schedule.

We use a classic device for this: office hours. Several times a week I set aside time at the end of the day to meet with founders. Because these meetings come at the end of my working day, they're never an interruption.

## The Speculative Meeting Problem

On the manager's schedule, you can afford to have speculative meetings — you have an empty slot, why not? Maybe something good will come of it. Business people in Silicon Valley have these all the time. They call it "grabbing coffee."

But on the maker's schedule, speculative meetings are terribly costly. This creates a bind. Everyone assumes investors use the manager's schedule, so they propose meetings or introductions. You have two bad options: meet them and lose half a day, or decline and probably offend them.

Now that I've articulated this, perhaps a third option exists: write an essay explaining the two types of schedule. If the conflict becomes more widely understood, it will become less of a problem.

Those of us on the maker's schedule are willing to compromise. We know some meetings are necessary. We just ask that people on the manager's schedule understand the cost.
