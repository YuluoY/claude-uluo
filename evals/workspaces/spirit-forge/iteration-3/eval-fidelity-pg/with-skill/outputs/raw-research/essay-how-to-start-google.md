# How to Start Google

By Paul Graham, March 2024

This is based on a talk I gave to 14- and 15-year-olds about what to do now if they might eventually want to start a startup.

After school, most people assume you have to get a job. But there's a trick to avoid that: start your own company. It's not about avoiding work — founders work harder than employees. It's about avoiding having a boss, and working on your own projects. It's also the standard way to get really rich.

## What Kind of Company

Starting a company spans a wide spectrum — from a barber shop to Google. I'm talking about the Google end: startups. My wife Jessica and I started Y Combinator in 2005, basically a startup factory. Since 2005 YC has funded over 4,000 startups.

You might think: "How could we start Google?" But Larry Page and Sergey Brin had no idea their company would be worth over a trillion dollars. When starting, you can't know whether you're building the next Google or something that fails.

## The Three Things You Need

To start a successful startup, you need: (1) skill at some technology, (2) an idea for what to build, and (3) cofounders.

### 1. Getting Good at Technology

Both how to get good at technology and which one to pursue have the same answer: work on your own projects. Don't try to predict whether gene editing, LLMs, or rockets will be valuable — no one can predict that. Pursue whatever interests you most.

If you're unsure, programming is the safe bet. It's been the source of the median startup for 30 years, and will be for at least another decade.

School CS classes aren't enough. You can ace classes and graduate from a top university without actually being good at programming. Tech companies test coding ability regardless of your background.

Real learning happens through personal projects. Building a game forces you to learn things you can't avoid, and you learn much faster. Technology broadly means anything you can make or build — welding, clothing, videos. The distinction is whether you're producing or consuming.

Steve Jobs studied calligraphy as a teenager. It seemed useless until the Macintosh needed beautiful typography — and Apple destroyed everyone at that.

Practice is the key. Start programming at 15, found a company at 22, and you'll have 7 years of practice. You can get very good at anything in that time.

### 2. Getting Startup Ideas

Once you're skilled at technology, ideas become easy. You start seeing dotted outlines around the things that are missing — gaps in the technology itself and broken things it could fix.

A shop has a sign warning that its door sticks, and it's been there for years. To the employees, it's a mysterious natural phenomenon. To a carpenter passing through, it's obvious: plane off the part that sticks.

Similarly, once you know programming, missing software becomes obvious. Before Facebook, Harvard had printed "facebooks" — directories with student photos. In 2002, there was no online version. The administration was meeting about it and might solve it in another decade. Zuckerberg thought: "This is stupid. I could write a program to fix this in one night." He did, and had thousands of users almost overnight.

Apple wasn't meant to be a company. Wozniak just wanted to build his own computer. Jobs proposed selling plans. They started selling plans, not even actual computers. Can you imagine how lame this seemed?

Google: before them, search engines returned every page containing a query term, without ranking by importance. The web was growing exponentially, making this approach exponentially more broken. Most users accepted the hassle. Larry and Sergey thought: "Some pages about rugby matter more than others."

The best startups grow out of projects that weren't meant to be startups. For technically skilled young people, unconscious instincts about what's interesting are better than conscious ideas about what would be a good company.

Build things for yourself and your friends. If you make something your friends genuinely want — something they'd be sad to lose — you have the kernel of a good startup. It may not seem like a startup. Trust me, there's a way.

### 3. Finding Cofounders

The optimal startup has 2 or 3 founders. The way to find them is the same as everything else: work on projects together.

School performance matters because grades affect university admission. You should try to get into the best university you can, because that's where the best cofounders are. Larry and Sergey hired the smartest people they knew out of Stanford, giving them a real advantage.

The largest numbers of successful startups come from the most selective universities. It's not about brand names or teaching quality. It's about how hard they are to get into — you have to be pretty smart and determined to get into MIT.

Strangely, this effect is strongest in the US where admissions are flawed and require jumping through arbitrary hoops. The more arbitrary the test, the more it selects for determination and resourcefulness — the two most important founder qualities.

## Conclusion

The path boils down to two things: build stuff and do well in school. Get good at technology through personal projects. Excel academically to reach a good university where cofounders and ideas await.
