# Do Things That Don't Scale

By Paul Graham, July 2013

At Y Combinator, we advise founders to do things that don't scale. Many startup founders believe that "startups either take off or they don't." They think if you build something great, users will flock to it. But actually, startups take off because the founders make them take off.

Startups are like old cars with a crank handle on the front. Once the engine starts, the fuel mixture powers itself. But there's a separate, labor-intensive process to get it going.

## Recruiting Users Manually

The most common unscalable thing founders must do is recruit users manually. Nearly all startups have to. You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them.

Stripe used what we call the "Collison installation" technique. Instead of sending a link, they would walk up to people and set them up immediately. If someone agreed to try Stripe, the founders would say "Alright, give me your laptop" and set it up right there.

Founders resist manual recruiting for two reasons: shyness or laziness, and the deceptive smallness of initial numbers. They can't imagine going from 100 users to millions. But growth compounds. If you start with 100 users and grow 10% a week, you'll have 14,000 users in a year and 2 million in two years.

Airbnb is the classic example. The founders went door-to-door in New York, recruiting hosts and teaching them to take better photos. They'd fly to New York and arrive at YC dinners directly from the airport with their rolly bags.

## Delight Your Users

You should take extraordinary measures to make early users happy. Wufoo sent handwritten thank-you notes to every new user. Why don't more founders do this? Three reasons: engineering training doesn't prepare you for customer service, they worry it won't scale, and they've never experienced such attention themselves as customers.

But you can provide a level of service no big company can. If you're worried about scaling this, having too many users to delight by hand is a great problem to have.

## The Experience, Not the Product

For early-stage startups, the product doesn't need to be great — the experience of being your user does. You can have a buggy, incomplete product and still deliver an insanely great experience through attentiveness. Over-engaging with early users both drives growth and feeds the product development loop.

## Contained Fire

Sometimes the right unscalable approach is focusing on a narrow market first. Keep a fire contained to get it really hot before adding more logs. Facebook started only for Harvard students. Marketplaces almost always need to start within a geographic subset.

## Manual Backends

An extreme variant: do things manually that you plan to automate later. This lets you launch faster, and you'll know exactly what to build because you've done it yourself. Stripe's "instant" merchant accounts were originally created by founders manually signing up users behind the scenes.

## The Vector Model

Think of startup ideas not as scalars but as vectors: a pair of what you're going to build, plus the unscalable things you'll do initially. In the best case, those unscalable efforts become part of the company's DNA. If you work hard to delight users when you only have a handful, you'll keep doing it when you have a lot.
