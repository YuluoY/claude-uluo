# Default Alive or Default Dead

By Paul Graham, October 2015

When I talk to a startup that's been operating for more than 8 or 9 months, the first thing I want to know is: assuming their expenses stay flat and their revenue growth stays what it's been over the last several months, do they make it to profitability on the money they have left? Or put more dramatically: by default do they live or die?

## The Startling Discovery

Half the founders I talk to don't know the answer to this question. I point them to a calculator Trevor Blackwell wrote.

## Why This Matters

If you're default alive, we can talk about ambitious new things. If you're default dead, and we should probably be talking about how to save you from dying.

## Why Founders Don't Know

Mainly because they haven't been asking. Early on, asking whether you're default alive is like asking a 3-year-old how he plans to support himself. The question goes from meaningless to critical as the company grows, and this transition catches people by surprise.

Better to start worrying too early about being default dead than too late. Because there's a thing I wrote about called the fatal pinch: default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix it.

## The Fundraising Assumption

Another reason founders don't know: they assume raising more money will be easy. But that assumption is often wrong, and the more you depend on it, the falser it becomes.

You should separate facts from hopes. Say explicitly: "We're default dead, but we're counting on investors to save us." When you actually hear those words, you realize what a risky position you're in.

## Growth vs. Spending

There is surprisingly little connection between how much a startup spends and how fast it grows. Fast growth means your product hits a big need directly. High spending typically means your product is expensive to develop or sell, or you're wasteful.

## How to Avoid Being Default Dead

The number one answer: don't hire too fast. Hiring too fast is by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money.

### Why Founders Overhire

- They see volume of work and think hiring will fix it
- Successful companies have big staffs — but that's more the effect of growth than the cause
- Most dangerously: when growth is slow, they don't want to face that the product isn't appealing enough

VCs often encourage overhiring because they want kill-or-cure — their portfolios protect them. But you want above all to survive.

### The Death Spiral

A startup makes something moderately appealing, raises money easily because the founders are smart and the idea is plausible, growth is okay but not great, they hire more to boost growth, but it doesn't come because the product is only moderately appealing. Now they're burning more and more, and they die.

### The Real Fix

Address the fundamental problem: the product is only moderately appealing. Hiring rarely fixes that. At early stages, the product needs to evolve more than to be "built out" — and that's easier with fewer people.

## The Alternative

Asking the default alive/dead question might counteract the pressure to overhire. Instead, founders might seek growth by doing things that don't scale, or redesigning the product in ways only founders can. For most, these paths actually work.

Airbnb waited 4 months after Y Combinator before hiring their first employee. The founders were terribly overworked, but they were overworked evolving Airbnb into the astonishingly successful organism it became.
