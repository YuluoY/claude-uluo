# Why AI Startups Must Be Default Alive

A few weeks ago I talked to a founder who'd just raised $8 million. When I asked about his runway, he said "about 18 months." This is the standard answer. But then I asked the question I always ask: "Are you default alive?"

He didn't know what I meant.

"Default alive" means your revenue covers your costs and your growth comes from your own cash flow. If you shut off fundraising tomorrow, you survive. Default dead means you need another round to stay afloat. Most startups are default dead. This is not a problem for most startups. For AI startups, it's existential.

Why? Because AI startups have a cost structure problem that other startups don't. A SaaS company's marginal cost is near zero — add one more user, the server cost rounds to nothing. An AI startup's marginal cost is real — every query burns GPU time, every generation costs tokens. The more customers you get, the more you pay. This is not how software businesses are supposed to work.

Which means the standard playbook — grow fast, figure out unit economics later — doesn't apply. If you grow 10% a week with negative margins, you are not growing a business. You are burning a pile of money on a timer.

I saw this pattern at Y Combinator with the GPT-wrapper startups. Beautiful demos. Overnight user growth. And underneath it all, API bills that grew exactly as fast as the user base. The founders were building a toll bridge where the toll was less than the cost of collecting it.

The AI startups that survive will be the ones that solve this at the architecture level. The obvious fix is to make your model smaller and more efficient. Run it on-device. Distill it. Fine-tune a 7B model to do one thing extremely well instead of calling a 400B model to do everything passably.

But there's a less obvious fix that matters more: charge real money.

Most AI founders are terrified of pricing. They saw what happened when ChatGPT launched for free. They think the only way to compete is to give away the product and hope to monetize later. This is wrong. If you're building something people genuinely need, they will pay for it. If they won't pay for it, you haven't built something they need. You've built a demo.

Airbnb didn't compete with free couches. They competed with hotels that cost $200 a night — and won because their product was better. The price wasn't the story. The product was.

The good news is that AI startups actually have an advantage here that SaaS companies don't. SaaS products have been devalued over two decades — there are 30,000 of them, and most are commodities with a subscription form. AI products are new. Customers don't have a fixed price anchor yet. You get to set the expectation.

The bad news is that this window is closing. Every month that passes, more AI products launch with free tiers, and the price of intelligence trends toward zero. If you don't establish that your product costs money now, you never will.

There's a deeper reason this matters. Startups that are default dead optimize for the wrong thing. They optimize for the next fundraise — the vanity metrics, the growth-hack numbers, the narrative that VCs want to hear. Startups that are default alive optimize for their customers. They have to. Customers are the only source of money.

For AI startups specifically, being default alive forces something else: it forces you to build an AI product that is so useful that users will pay for it despite free alternatives. The bar is higher, but the result is a real business.

The default should be alive. For AI startups, it has to be.
