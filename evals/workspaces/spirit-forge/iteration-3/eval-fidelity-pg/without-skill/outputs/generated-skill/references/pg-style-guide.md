# Paul Graham Style Guide — Detailed Reference

This document provides a comprehensive stylistic reference for emulating Paul Graham's essay writing, compiled from analysis of 8+ essays and his own writing advice.

---

## 1. Sentence Architecture

### Length Distribution
PG mixes lengths strategically:
- **Short punches (3-12 words):** Used for thesis statements, paragraph closers, and emphasis. E.g., "Choose the latter." "They were." "Growth drives everything."
- **Medium exposition (13-25 words):** The workhorse sentences that carry most of the argument.
- **Long chains (26-50+ words):** Used for layered qualification or multi-step logic, often connected by dashes, colons, or semicolons.

Target average: **10-22 words per sentence.** Longer than that and you're lecturing; shorter and you're telegraphing.

### Sentence Openers
PG frequently begins sentences with:
- "And" / "But" / "So" — conversational linking
- "Which means" — implication pivot
- "For example" — grounding abstraction  
- "The good news is" / "The bad news is" — concessive
- "In principle..." — setup for qualification
- "The fascinating thing about" — insight cue
- "If you..." — direct address hypothesis

### Closing Cadence
Paragraphs typically end on a short, definitive sentence that lands like a verdict. After 2-3 sentences of explanation, finish with a 5-8 word judgment.

## 2. Paragraph Architecture

### The Micro-Argument Pattern
Each paragraph is a self-contained unit:
```
Claim → Evidence/Analogy/Example → Implication or Verdict
```

### Length Rules
- **1 sentence:** Emphasis, transition, or verdict (use sparingly — 10-20% of paragraphs)
- **2-3 sentences:** The sweet spot for most argument steps
- **4-5 sentences:** Extended explanation, rare
- **6+ sentences:** Almost never — break it up

### Transition Patterns
PG rarely uses explicit transitions ("Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally"). Instead he uses:
- Short pivot paragraphs ("Which means...")
- Rhetorical questions ("But how does it work?" "Why?")
- Direct reframing ("Actually, X.")

## 3. Essay Architecture

### Opening Moves (Choose One)
1. **Personal anecdote:** "About a month after we started Y Combinator..."
2. **Contrarian reframing:** "The way to get X is not to try to get X."
3. **Paradox setup:** "If you combine the two standard pieces of advice for startups, you get a strange result."
4. **Direct statement of the problem:** "Everyone tells founders to grow fast. But no one tells them how."

### Middle Structure
- Flow from observation → principle → application
- Use section breaks for major pivots (PG sometimes uses numbered sections or simple horizontal separators)
- Each section explores one facet, typically 4-8 paragraphs
- Intersperse concrete examples, analogies, and counterintuitive reveals

### Closing Moves
- **Recursive return:** Circle back to the opening anecdote or concept, now with deeper meaning
- **Practical takeaway:** "If I had to choose one thing..."
- **Deliberate understatement:** End on a quiet note that lets the essay's argument speak for itself
- **Open door:** Leave the reader with a question or implication that extends beyond the essay

### The footnote layer
PG's footnotes serve as a second, more technical voice — they handle edge cases, self-corrections, historical asides, and self-deprecation. Use them sparingly (3-8 per essay) and make them conversational, not academic.

## 4. Vocabulary and Diction

### Preferred Lexicon
Anglo-Saxon, concrete, short:
- build, want, need, make, show, get, find, work, hard, fast, good, bad, small, big, cheap

### Avoided Lexicon
Latinate, abstract, long:
- utilize, demonstrate, facilitate, methodology, paradigm, leverage (verb), optimize (unless precise), regarding, furthermore, nevertheless

### Coinages
PG occasionally coins memorable terms through metaphor:
- "ramen profitable" (enough revenue to cover founders' food costs)
- "schlep blindness" (unwillingness to see tedious but valuable problems)
- "sitcom startup ideas" (ideas that sound plausible but don't actually work)
- "default alive" vs "default dead" (a startup's status without additional funding)

These work when they compress a complex concept into a sticky, intuitive phrase.

### Numbers as Vocabulary
Always prefer numeric specificity over adjectives:
- Not "very quickly" but "10% a week"
- Not "much more productive" but "36 times more productive"
- Not "most startups" but "about 70% of startups"

## 5. Rhetorical Devices — Usage Patterns

### Analogy (Primary Device)
- **Frequency:** 1 analogy per 3-5 paragraphs
- **Pattern:** Compare the unfamiliar to the familiar in a single sentence, then extend
- **Examples:** Startups = mosquitoes (designed to score a goal, no defense); Cofounders = real estate (you can change the paint but not the location)

### Reframing via Negation (Signature Move)
- **Pattern:** "The way to get X is not to try to get X. It's to..."
- **Purpose:** Clears away conventional approaches before presenting a better one

### Maxim-as-Axiom (Compression)
- **Pattern:** Condense the essay's thesis into a 5-10 word proverb and repeat it
- **Examples:** "Make something people want." "Live in the future, then build what's missing."

### Counterintuitive Reveal
- **Pattern:** State something counterintuitive, then spend 2-3 paragraphs justifying it
- **Purpose:** Keeps readers engaged, rewards attention

### Reductio ad Absurdum
- **Pattern:** "If you push X to its logical conclusion, you get Y — which is absurd. Therefore X is flawed."
- **Frequency:** 1-2 times per essay

### Self-Deprecation as Shield
- **Pattern:** "I am not a particularly good person" / "I got this wrong when I first wrote about it"
- **Purpose:** Disarms resistance after making bold claims

## 6. Tone Calibration Checklist

### What the tone IS:
- [ ] Conversational — sounds like talking, not writing
- [ ] Confident — unhedged declarative statements
- [ ] Curious — exploring a question, not defending a position
- [ ] Practical — grounded in specific examples and numbers
- [ ] Contrarian — challenging conventional wisdom
- [ ] Slightly self-deprecating — willing to admit past mistakes
- [ ] Mentor-like — "I made this mistake, here's what I learned"

### What the tone is NOT:
- [ ] Academic — no "the literature suggests," no jargon
- [ ] Journalistic — no inverted pyramid, no "both sides" framing
- [ ] Motivational — no rah-rah, no "you can do it!"
- [ ] Clinical — no emotional distance, use "I" and "you"
- [ ] Bureaucratic — no passive voice, no responsibility-dodging
- [ ] Self-help — no numbered steps to happiness
- [ ] Pop-intellectual — no Malcolm Gladwell-style narrative arcs with dramatic reveals

## 7. Anti-Patterns: The Complete List

These are the things PG never does in his essays:

1. **Hedges claims with "in my opinion" or "I think"** — he just makes the claim
2. **Uses the passive voice to avoid responsibility** — "it was decided" never appears
3. **Opens with broad historical context** — "Since the dawn of..." is alien to him
4. **Cites academic papers** — he references people, companies, and direct experience, not journals
5. **Uses jargon without immediately defining it** — technical terms are introduced and explained
6. **Writes paragraphs longer than 5 sentences** — walls of text are his enemy
7. **Uses "one" as a pronoun** — "One might think" becomes "You might think"
8. **Qualifies every statement for balance** — he commits to positions
9. **Relies on Latinate abstractions** — "commence" → "start," "terminate" → "end"
10. **Ends with a generic call to action** — his endings are recursive, not motivational
11. **Uses exclamation points** — emphasis comes from structure, not punctuation
12. **Includes social-media-friendly pull quotes** — his prose resists extraction; it works in context

## 8. The PG Voice Test

Before publishing any PG-style essay, run these checks:

1. Read the entire thing aloud. Add a comma everywhere you pause. Remove anything that sounds unnatural.

2. Check paragraph lengths. Count the sentences in each. More than 25% should be 1 sentence. None should exceed 5.

3. Count hedging phrases. If you find "I think," "in my opinion," "perhaps," "it seems," "one might argue" — delete them all. PG uses approximately zero per essay.

4. Find the thesis sentence. It should be under 12 words and appear in the first 3 paragraphs. If it's longer, compress it.

5. Check the opening. Does it start with a story, a paradox, or a contrarian reframe? If it starts with context or background, move it later or delete it.

6. Check the closing. Does it circle back to the opening? Does it leave the reader with an implication rather than a summary?

7. Count "which means" and "for example" — each should appear at natural pivot points, usually 3-5 times per essay.

8. Verify every abstract claim has a concrete anchor (a number, a company name, a person, an anecdote).

9. Count syllables in the first sentence of each paragraph. The average should be under 1.5 syllables per word. If it's higher, you're reaching for Latinate words.

10. Read it to someone. If they say "this sounds like you talking" — you have succeeded.
