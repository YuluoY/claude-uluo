# Paul Graham Essay Writer

Write essays that emulate the distinctive style of Paul Graham — the Y Combinator founder and essayist whose prose is known for radical simplicity, conversational authority, and contrarian insight.

## Core Style Principles

### 1. Write Like You Talk
Every sentence should pass the "would I say this to a friend?" test. Read everything aloud before publishing. If it doesn't sound like conversation, rewrite it. Informal language is the athletic clothing of ideas.

### 2. Radical Minimalism
Cut every unnecessary word. No hedging, no padding, no "in my opinion" or "it seems that." Say things you're sure of and say them plainly. The result will sound confident — some will call it arrogant. That's the price of precision.

### 3. Short Paragraphs
Most paragraphs should be 1-5 sentences. Single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis. No walls of text. Each paragraph advances one micro-argument or observation, then yields.

### 4. Conversational Authority
Use "you" extensively. Write as a practitioner-theorist — someone who's done the thing and now explains patterns. Be authoritative without being academic.

### 5. Contrarian Framing
Start by telling the reader what NOT to do before revealing what TO do. The most powerful hook is "actually, the conventional wisdom is wrong."

### 6. Analogy-Driven Reasoning
Turn abstract concepts into sticky mental models. Compare startups to mosquito biology, programming languages to religious habits, startup ideas to wells and holes. Each analogy should make the abstract instantly concrete.

### 7. Essay as Exploration
An essay is a search for truth, not a defense of a thesis. Start with a question or an observation that surprised you. Follow where it leads. If you don't learn something from writing it, don't publish it.

### 8. Precision Through Specificity
Use concrete numbers, specific examples, and named companies (Google, Apple, Airbnb). Don't say "many companies" when you can say "most YC startups." Numerical framing (7% a week, 36x more productive) anchors abstract claims in measurable reality.

## Paragraph Rhythm

Each paragraph follows a pattern: **claim → evidence → mini-conclusion**. Or a single-sentence punchline that lands like a verdict after longer explanatory paragraphs.

## Sentence Rhythm

Mix very short declarative sentences (5-12 words) with longer explanatory ones (25-50+ words). End paragraphs with a short, definitive sentence. Vary the rhythm: assertion, elaboration, shorter reassertion.

## Signature Moves

- **Opening with personal anecdote** then building to universal principle
- **"Which means"** — pivot from observation to implication
- **"For example"** — grounding abstraction in concrete instance
- **"The good news is / The bad news is"** — concessive structure
- **"In principle... But"** — establish then qualify
- **"The fascinating thing about"** — cue that insight is coming
- **Deliberate repetition of key terms** — embed the thesis through frequency
- **Reframing via negation**: "The way to get X is not to try to get X. It's to..."
- **Maxim-as-axiom**: compress complex ideas into pseudo-proverbial formulations
- **Recursive closing**: circle back to the opening, now with deeper meaning
- **Rhetorical questions** as structural pivots between sections
- **Cautionary confession**: "I made this mistake, so you don't have to"
- **Reductio ad absurdum**: push a common belief to its logical extreme to reveal absurdity

### Vocabulary Choices

- Colloquial, Anglo-Saxon-root words over Latinate abstractions: "build" not "construct," "need" not "require," "hard" not "difficult"
- Precise numerical framing when possible ("5-7% a week")
- Deliberate repetition of the essay's key concept (the word appears dozens of times)
- Introduce technical terms only to immediately define them through analogy

## What to Avoid

1. **Jargon and academic vocabulary** — "utilize" → "use," "demonstrate" → "show"
2. **Hedging phrases** — no "it seems that," "one might argue," "in my opinion"
3. **Phatic social padding** — don't reassure readers that you're a nice person or might be wrong
4. **Long impersonal sentences** — no passive voice ("it was decided"), use "I" and "you"
5. **Walls of text** — break up anything over 5 sentences
6. **Writing to impress** — if it sounds fancy, it's hiding a lack of ideas
7. **Academic citation style** — use footnotes sparingly, conversationally
8. **Abstract nouns without concrete examples** — every claim needs a tangible instance
9. **Thesaurus words** — don't reach for "ameliorate" when you mean "fix"
10. **Starting with broad context** — start with a specific observation and work outward

## Gotchas: PG-Specific Writing Rules

These are the non-obvious rules that separate good imitations from actual PG style. Violating any of them breaks the illusion.

### 1. Never Hedge — State Everything as Confident Knowledge
PG never writes "I think maybe" or "it's possible that" when he could simply assert the thing. He writes from the pose of full knowledge, as if stating timeless truths. If you're unsure about something, you don't express uncertainty — you either omit it entirely or reframe it as a question the essay is exploring. The Morris technique: never say anything unless you're sure it's worth hearing. This means you kill uncertain sentences, not qualify them.

**Wrong:** "In my opinion, most startups fail because of co-founder conflict."
**Right:** "Most startups fail because of co-founder conflict."

### 2. The Phatic Layer Must Be Excised Completely
Most writing contains a hidden thread whose purpose is to reassure the reader that the author is a nice person, open to being wrong, and not hostile. PG deliberately removes this entire layer. The result feels provocative — readers accustomed to social cushioning interpret its absence as aggression. Every sentence must earn its place by advancing the argument or illuminating the idea. No social lubricant.

**Wrong:** "While I understand that many people feel differently about this, and I respect those views..."
**Right:** [Delete the sentence entirely.]

### 3. The First Draft Must Be Conversational — Polish Comes Later
PG writes first drafts fast and loose, as if speaking. The revision process then tightens without formalizing. The final text should still sound like someone talking at a cafe, not someone delivering a lecture. If a sentence sounds like it belongs in The Economist, kill it. The test: read every sentence aloud. If you stumble, or if it sounds like writing instead of speech, rewrite.

**Wrong:** "Entrepreneurs should endeavor to ascertain market requirements prior to product development."
**Right:** "Don't build something and hope people want it. Find out first."

### 4. Use Concrete Examples, Not Abstract Categories
PG never says "many technology companies utilize agile methodologies." He says "Google doesn't use waterfall." Every claim must be anchored to a specific, named entity — a company, a person, a number. This is what makes his essays feel grounded rather than theoretical. When you catch yourself writing "some companies" or "many founders," replace it with a specific name or a specific number you can defend.

**Wrong:** "Certain high-growth enterprises exhibit patterns that..."
**Right:** "Airbnb grew 10% a week for their first year. This is not unusual."

### 5. No Tapestry Paragraphs — One Idea Per Paragraph
PG's paragraphs are radically short because each one carries exactly one idea. If a paragraph contains two distinct claims, split it. A paragraph of 6+ sentences almost certainly needs to be broken. The goal is for each paragraph to be a self-contained unit of thought — a single punch rather than a combination.

**Wrong:** [Paragraphs longer than 5 sentences that blend multiple micro-arguments]
**Right:** [Short, atomic paragraphs, sometimes just one sentence]

### 6. The Essay Must Surprise Its Author
PG's central test for whether an essay is worth publishing: did you learn something while writing it? If the essay merely confirms what you already knew, it's not an essay — it's a blog post, a manifesto, or a lecture. Real essays are acts of discovery. You should start with a question, not a thesis. The argument should meander, backtrack, and end somewhere you didn't expect.

### 7. Avoid Latinate Words When Anglo-Saxon Ones Exist
PG's vocabulary is deliberately plain. "Use" not "utilize." "Need" not "require." "Show" not "demonstrate." "Start" not "commence." "Build" not "construct." The default should always be the shorter, more common word. Reach for the longer one only when the shorter one genuinely doesn't convey the right nuance — which is rare. If you find yourself choosing a word because it sounds more sophisticated, choose the plainer one instead.

**Wrong:** "This methodology facilitates rapid iteration."
**Right:** "This lets you iterate fast."

### 8. Numbers, Not Adjectives
Quantify everything you can. Don't say "extraordinary growth" when you can say "7% a week." Don't say "much more productive" when you can say "36 times more productive." Precise numbers do two things: they make claims falsifiable (which forces you to be honest), and they make abstract concepts emotionally real in a way adjectives never can.

**Wrong:** "Startups need to grow extremely quickly to matter."
**Right:** "A good growth rate is 5-7% a week. At 5% you're on track. At 7% you're doing exceptionally well. Below 2% — you're not a startup."

## Tone Calibration

The ideal PG essay reads like someone who has thought about something deeply for years, then sat down and explained it to a smart friend over coffee. It's conversational but never sloppy. It's confident but not preachy. It makes bold claims but earns the right to do so through clear thinking and specific evidence. It ends by circling back to the beginning, now with deeper meaning, leaving the reader with something they can use.
