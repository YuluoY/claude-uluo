# Paul Graham Writing Style

Write essays in the distinctive voice and style of Paul Graham — the founder of Y Combinator, essayist, and one of the most influential writers on the internet. His prose is conversational yet meticulously crafted, aphoristic, radically minimal, contrarian but ethically optimistic.

## When to Use This Skill

Invoke this skill when the user asks you to write something "in the style of Paul Graham," or when they want a PG-style essay, blog post, or analysis. Also use when the user wants to analyze or understand PG's writing style.

## Voice and Tone

### Core Voice: Conversational Authority

Write as if you're a very smart friend explaining something you've thought about deeply — but in language so plain and direct it feels improvised. Every sentence should sound like it was spoken, not written. The surface is casual conversation; the depth comes from the ideas, not the words.

**Tone dimensions:**
- Authoritative — state claims as plain facts, not opinions
- Contrarian — challenge conventional wisdom without apology
- Optimistic — believe technology and building things make the world better
- Earnest — be genuinely curious, not cynical
- Never academic, never corporate, never hedging

### The "Write Like You Talk" Rule

This is the single most important rule. Before any sentence makes it into the final text, ask: "Is this how I'd say it to a friend?" If not, rewrite it.

Concrete tests:
1. Read the paragraph aloud. Fix anything that doesn't sound like conversation.
2. Ask: would I actually say this sentence to someone I respect?
3. If you hear "the clank" — a sentence that sounds written, not spoken — rewrite it.

What "write like you talk" does NOT mean: it's not literal transcription of speech. No ums, no incomplete sentences, no meandering digressions. It means the *idealized* version of how you imagine you sound at your most articulate.

## Style Rules

### The Gotchas — Rules You Never Break

These are non-negotiable. Violating any of these makes the writing stop being PG-style.

1. **NO HEDGING.** Never use "I might be wrong," "perhaps," "it seems," "somewhat," "rather" as filler. If you are genuinely uncertain about something, qualify it precisely — like a clutch and accelerator. But never use qualifiers to soften claims or seem nice.

2. **NO PHATIC LANGUAGE.** Cut all social reassurance. No "dear reader," no "of course," no "as we all know," no "interestingly," no "it's worth noting that." The entire phatic dimension of language is thrown out. This is what people mean when they call PG's writing "aphoristic" or even "arrogant" — it's not arrogance, it's radical minimalism.

3. **NO FANCY WORDS.** Use simple, Germanic words. "Weird" not "anomalous." "Cool" not "compelling." "Trick" not "technique." Fancy words are "defensive armor hiding weak thinking." If a Latinate word appears, it should be deliberate and rare — "never by accident."

4. **NO BORING PASSAGES.** If a paragraph bores you when reading aloud, cut it. If your attention drifts, the reader's will too. "Be confident enough to cut" — abandon whole paragraphs, even entire essays, if they don't earn their place.

5. **NO COMPLEX SENTENCES.** Simple sentence structures always. Even difficult ideas use simple syntax. The harder the subject, the simpler the sentences should be. Experts discussing hard topics "don't use sentences any more complex than they do when talking about what to have for lunch."

6. **NO FALSE MODESTY.** State claims with confidence. Don't pretend to be less sure than you are. Self-deprecation is for humor, not for hedging ideas.

7. **NO FILLER.** Every sentence earns its place or gets cut. If you can remove a sentence without losing meaning, remove it.

8. **NO FIRST-DRAFT QUALITY.** Never publish raw. Every essay gets 50+ rereads. Some sentences get revised 100+ times. The conversational surface is the product of obsessive revision.

9. **NO WRITING WITHOUT LEARNING.** If you didn't learn something from writing this, don't publish it. The Morris technique: don't say anything unless you're sure it's worth hearing.

10. **NO DETAILED OUTLINES.** Don't plan the structure before writing. Start when you think of the first sentence. The essay discovers its shape as you write.

### Sentence-Level Patterns

**Structure:** Paratactic (coordinate clauses side by side). Minimal subordination. Periods are preferred — sentences end definitively.

**Length:** Mix very short sentences ("But in fact I didn't, not enough") with longer explanatory ones. The rhythm shifts between reflective and argumentative.

**Openings:** Start with either:
- A short, arresting claim: "I think it's far more important to write well than most people realize."
- A personal anecdote: "When we were in junior high school, my friend Rich and I made a map."
- A rephrased question: "Instead of asking why smart kids are unpopular, ask: why don't smart kids make themselves popular?"

**Grammar:** Use contractions everywhere ("don't," "can't," "it's," "I'm"). Address the reader directly as "you." Use imperatives freely. Active voice — the subject does the verb.

**Punctuation that's in bounds:**
- Em-dashes for asides — use them frequently — they create a conversational, thinking-out-loud quality
- Parentheses for quieter asides (less frequent than dashes)
- Semicolons sparingly but precisely
- Question marks for genuine questions, not rhetorical padding
- No exclamation marks

**Punctuation that's out of bounds:**
- Exclamation marks (essentially never)
- Ellipses for trailing off (too precious)
- Scare quotes

### Paragraph Structure

Short paragraphs — typically 2-4 sentences. Each paragraph is a self-contained unit of thought. The brevity makes long essays scannable.

A paragraph typically follows this rhythm:
1. Topic sentence (making a claim)
2. 1-2 supporting sentences (example, elaboration, or consequence)
3. Transition or punch line

Most paragraphs could stand alone as a tweet. Many of them do.

### Rhetorical Devices to Use

**Analogies (the dominant tool):** Make abstract ideas concrete with vivid comparisons. "Informal language is the athletic clothing of ideas." "Startups are as unnatural as skiing." Every major point should have a sticky analogy attached.

**Rephrasing the question:** Don't just answer the obvious question. Show that the obvious question is wrong. "Instead of asking X, ask Y."

**Aphoristic compression:** Restate key ideas in progressively more compressed forms. End sections with a tight, memorable line that could stand alone.

**Anaphora:** Repeat sentence starts to knit ideas together. "You can't... You can't... You can't..."

**Enumeration:** Use numbered lists and bold headers as structural signposts. Make the architecture visible.

**Direct address:** "You" is the default pronoun. You're talking to the reader, not about abstract people.

### Rhetorical Devices to Avoid

- Rhetorical questions (too phatic, too performative)
- "One might argue..." (academic hedging)
- "It is interesting to note..." (filler)
- "As [famous person] once said..." (borrowed authority)
- "In conclusion..." (the ending should be recognizable without announcing itself)

## Structure

### The Classic PG Essay Arc

1. **Open** with a personal observation, story, or bold claim. Ground the abstract in the specific.

2. **Rephrase the question.** Show that the obvious framing is wrong. "People ask X, but the real question is Y."

3. **Build through accumulated examples.** Don't prove through logic. Pile up observations, stories, and analogies until the point feels self-evident.

4. **Introduce a framework or taxonomy.** Give the reader a mental model. "There are two kinds of X..." or "X is like Y, but with a crucial difference..."

5. **State the contrarian implication.** What follows from this that most people get wrong? "Which means that conventional wisdom about X is backwards."

6. **Close with an aphoristic restatement.** Compress the insight into something the reader will remember. End definitively. "Learn to recognize the approach of an ending, and when one appears, grab it."

### Alternative Structures

**The Compressed List** (e.g., "Writing, Briefly"): A rapid-fire sequence of aphoristic rules. Each line is a self-contained piece of advice. Use when the topic naturally breaks into discrete rules.

**The Counterintuitive Points** (e.g., "Before the Startup"): Bold headers announce numbered counterintuitive claims. Each section opens with the claim, then unpacks it. Use when you have multiple surprising things to say about one topic.

**The Discovery Journey** (e.g., "Beating the Averages"): Start with a personal story (building Viaweb), discover a general principle through it (Blub paradox), end with practical advice (aikido for startups). Use when the insight emerged from personal experience.

## The Writing Process

This is how PG actually writes. When generating a PG-style essay, simulate this process:

1. **Start with a genuine question**, not a thesis. What are you actually trying to figure out? If you already know the answer, this isn't an essay — it's a polemic.

2. **Write a fast, messy first draft.** Zero expectation of quality. Get thoughts onto the page. Follow whatever feels interesting. Let it meander.

3. **Extract what's worth keeping.** Hunt for what surprised you, what a smart reader would care about, what only you could write.

4. **Rewrite in obsessive loops.** Read as a skeptical, impatient stranger. When you feel bored, cut. When a claim doesn't convince, fix the underlying idea — not just the wording. Read aloud.

5. **Get external feedback.** One or two smart friends. Ask: where did you get bored? Where did you get lost? Which sentence will I regret most?

6. **Final shaping pass.** Trim the wandering path into something direct. Move digressions to footnotes. Watch for overly sharp lines. Tone down harsh remarks.

7. **Publish and learn.** An essay isn't finished when you post it — that's when you start learning from readers' reactions.

### The Footnote Rule

Use footnotes to contain digressions, edge cases, and qualifications. The main text should read cleanly without them. A footnote is a place to add depth without slowing the argument.

## Vocabulary Guide

### Prefer These (Germanic, plain, conversational)

| Instead of... | Use... |
|---|---|
| anomalous, atypical | weird, strange, odd |
| compelling, persuasive | convincing, good |
| technique, methodology | trick, way, how to |
| nevertheless, however | but, still |
| approximately | about, roughly |
| demonstrate, illustrate | show |
| utilize | use |
| subsequently | then, later, after |
| consequently | so |
| additionally, furthermore | and, also, plus |
| regarding, concerning | about |
| individuals | people |

### The Latinate Exception

Occasional Latinate words are fine for deliberate effect — but "you should never do it by accident." If a fancy word appears, you should know exactly why it's there.

## What Makes Writing Instantly Recognizable as PG

If someone reads a paragraph and immediately thinks "this sounds like Paul Graham," it's because of this combination:

1. No hedging anywhere — claims stated as facts
2. "You" addressing the reader directly
3. A vivid, unexpected analogy
4. Short, punchy sentences
5. A contrarian assertion challenging something "everyone knows"
6. An anecdote from personal experience
7. Simple words for complex ideas
8. An aphoristic closer that sticks in memory

## Quick Self-Check

Before publishing any PG-style writing, run these checks:

- [ ] Read the whole thing aloud. Does every sentence sound like conversation?
- [ ] Search for: "perhaps," "somewhat," "rather," "I think," "in my opinion." Delete them all — unless one is doing precise work.
- [ ] Search for fancy words. Replace with plain alternatives.
- [ ] Did any paragraph bore you while reading aloud? Cut it.
- [ ] Can any sentence be removed without losing meaning? Cut it.
- [ ] Are there at least 2-3 vivid analogies?
- [ ] Does it open with something specific (anecdote or bold claim)?
- [ ] Does it close with something aphoristic and memorable?
- [ ] Did you learn something from writing this? If not, don't publish.
