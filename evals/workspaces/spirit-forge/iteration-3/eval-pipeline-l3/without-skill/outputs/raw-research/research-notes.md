# Paul Graham Writing Style — Research Notes

## 1. Core Philosophy

### Writing as Discovery, Not Communication
- ~80% of ideas about a topic emerge **after** he starts writing
- ~50% of ideas he starts with turn out to be wrong
- "An essay is something you write *to figure something out*, not to dress up a conclusion you already have"
- Writing "doesn't just communicate ideas; it generates them"
- If he didn't learn anything from writing an essay, he won't publish it (the Morris technique)
- He wrote in notebooks for ~15 years before publishing anything

### Useful Writing = Importance x Novelty x Correctness x Strength
- **Correctness**: must be true, but precision matters — vagueness makes correctness easy
- **Strength**: claims as strong as possible without becoming false. "Bold, but true"
- **Importance**: number of people it matters to, times how much it matters to them. Use self as proxy
- **Novelty**: tell people something they didn't already know. Surprise them or articulate the unconscious
- These are multiplicative — weakness in any dimension destroys overall usefulness

### "Write Like You Talk"
- The single most important rule
- Written language is more complex (harder to read), more formal (reader's attention drifts), and deceptive (writer thinks they're saying more)
- Experts discussing hard topics use *simpler* sentences than when discussing lunch
- Informal language = "the athletic clothing of ideas"
- Test: read every sentence aloud — if you wouldn't say it to a friend, rewrite it
- Over time, you "hear the clank" when an unnatural sentence hits the page
- Nuance: it's "write like you *imagine* you talk" — idealized conversation, not transcription with ums and digressions

### Radical Minimalism
- "Cut out everything unnecessary"
- "Be confident enough to cut" — he abandons whole branches of 4–5 paragraphs, sometimes entire essays
- If bored reading a passage aloud, the reader will be too — cut it
- Simple writing "keeps you honest" — if you say nothing plainly, both you and readers see it
- Simple writing ages better (future readers are like non-native speakers today)
- Primary motivation: complicated sentences strike him as "clumsy," not fancy

## 2. Distinctive Stylistic Features

### 2.1 The "Aphatic" Dimension (Most Important Distinction)
- Coined by HN user **gruseom**: PG's writing is "radically aphatic"
- **Phatic language** = social dimension of communication (reassurance, hedging, "I might be wrong," "dear reader")
- PG deliberately removes ALL phatic content
- This includes: hedging words ("perhaps," "somewhat," "rather"), social niceties, signals that the author is "a nice guy," softening qualifiers
- Result: prose feels unusually direct, sometimes arrogant — but it's a stylistic choice, not personality
- PG confirms: "I try to cut every unnecessary word... If you want an essay of dense ideas to be readable, you have to be so curt you sound arrogant"
- PG adds: "there's no alternative" — dense ideas + readability require aggressive cutting
- Readers commit a "reverse ad hominem" — they map the aphatic style onto the author's personality

### 2.2 Conversational Yet Meticulously Crafted
- Sounds like a smart friend talking to you — but revised 50+ times
- Contractions everywhere ("don't," "can't," "it's")
- Direct address ("you") is constant
- Dashes and parenthetical asides create thinking-out-loud quality
- This creates a paradox: conversationally casual surface, obsessively crafted underneath

### 2.3 Short Paragraphs, Short Sentences
- Typical paragraph: 2-4 sentences
- Blog-post cadence — scannable, punchy
- Paratactic structure: simple statements side by side, minimal subordination
- Very short paragraphs compensate for essay length
- Each paragraph is a self-contained unit of thought

### 2.4 Personal Anecdote → Universal Principle → Actionable Insight
- Signature structural pattern
- Opens with a specific observation or story (friend visiting, making a map in junior high, building Viaweb)
- Builds outward to a broader universal principle
- Ends with something concrete the reader can act on
- Example from "Before the Startup": being a parent wanting to tell kids what he's learned → six counterintuitive points → "just learn"
- Example from "Beating the Averages": building Viaweb in Lisp → Blub paradox → aikido for startups

### 2.5 Analogy-Driven Reasoning
- Constant vivid analogies make abstract ideas concrete
- "Informal language is the athletic clothing of ideas"
- Startups are "as unnatural as skiing"
- Technology spreads like a "fractal stain"
- Programming languages are "half technology and half religion"
- School popularity is a "zero-sum game"
- Teenagers are "neurotic lapdogs" vs. Renaissance apprentices who were "working dogs"
- Blub paradox: programmers at level N can see below but not above
- These analogies function as sticky mental models

### 2.6 Provocative, Contrarian Assertions
- States claims as plain facts, not opinions
- No hedging, no "in my opinion," no "I think" unless precise about uncertainty
- This is the "classical style" (Thomas & Turner): truth presented as if it needs no argument, only accurate presentation
- Novelty + strength = some people will get mad. This is accepted as inevitable
- "People who disagree rarely disagree with what you've actually written"
- Countermove: ask critics to quote a specific passage they believe is false and explain why — "they never do"

### 2.7 Aphoristic Density
- Many sentences are standalone maxims, easily quotable
- "Nerds don't care about glamour, so to them the appeal of New York is a mystery"
- "If you're bad at writing and don't like to do it, you'll miss out on most of the ideas writing would have generated"
- "Just learn"
- "Don't let 'work' mean something other people tell you to do"
- Restating ideas in progressively more compressed forms
- Bold headers function as stand alone theses

### 2.8 Plain, Germanic Vocabulary
- Simple, Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ones
- "Weird," "cool," "nerds," "trick" alongside conceptual terms
- No jargon, no academic vocabulary
- Deliberate choice to lower barrier to entry while discussing sophisticated ideas
- Occasional Latinate word for deliberate effect — "never by accident"

### 2.9 Ethical Optimism (Underlying Tone)
- Blunt, contrarian surface; upbeat, morally optimistic underneath
- Technology creates value, building things is good, the world can be improved
- This prevents provocativeness from tipping into cynicism
- Balance: high ambition + pragmatic self-awareness

### 2.10 Footnotes as Counterpoint
- Uses footnotes extensively to add nuance without cluttering the main argument
- Digressions, qualifications, edge cases go into footnotes
- Main text stays clean and brisk

### 2.11 Repetition and Anaphora
- Explicitly recommends using anaphora (repeated sentence starts) to "knit sentences together"
- Key phrases recur throughout essays as thematic anchors
- Repetition creates rhythm and memorability

## 3. Gotchas — Rules PG Never Breaks

### Core Gotchas (Violating These Makes Writing "Not PG")

1. **NO HEDGING** — Never uses "I might be wrong," "perhaps," "it seems" as filler. Qualification is only used with precision ("accelerator and clutch"). This is the #1 gotcha.

2. **NO PHATIC LANGUAGE** — Never includes social reassurance. No "dear reader," no "of course," no "as we all know," no softening of claims to seem nice. The entire phatic dimension is thrown out.

3. **NO IMPRESSIVE/FANCY WORDS** — Never uses complex vocabulary to sound smart. "Don't try to sound impressive." Germanic words over Latinate ones. Fancy words are "defensive armor hiding weak thinking."

4. **NO BORING PASSAGES** — If a paragraph bores him when reading aloud, it's cut. "If you're bored reading a passage, your reader will be too."

5. **NO DETAILED OUTLINES** — Never starts with a rigid structure. Writing is discovery, not execution of a plan. Start writing when you think of the first sentence.

6. **NO COMPLEX SENTENCES FOR COMPLEXITY'S SAKE** — Simple sentence structures always. Even difficult ideas are expressed with simple syntax. "The harder the subject, the more informally experts speak."

7. **NO FALSE MODESTY** — States claims with confidence. No self-deprecating qualifiers about the quality of his own thinking. Self-deprecation is reserved for humor, not for hedging claims.

8. **NO FILLER** — Every sentence earns its place. If something can be cut without losing meaning, it's cut. He abandons whole 4-5 paragraph branches.

9. **NO PASSIVE VOICE (unless deliberate)** — Active, direct constructions. The subject does the verb.

10. **NO UNEARNED AUTHORITY** — Citations of historical figures are sparse and strategic. Doesn't namedrop to borrow credibility. Arguments stand on their own.

11. **NO FIRST DRAFT PUBLISHING** — Never publishes without intensive rewriting. Typical essay: ~2 weeks, 50+ rereads, some sentences revised 100+ times.

12. **NO WRITING WITHOUT LEARNING** — If he didn't learn something from writing the essay, he won't publish it.

### Process-Level Gotchas

13. **Never writes in short time quanta** — "Work in fairly big quanta of time." Not five minutes here and there.

14. **Never starts cold** — When restarting, always rereads what he has so far.

15. **Never forces coverage** — Accumulates notes at the bottom of the file but "don't feel obliged to cover any of them."

16. **Never trusts his own judgment alone** — Always gets external readers before publishing.

## 4. Process Pipeline

### Phase 1: Raw Material Collection
- Carry a small notebook or scrap paper
- Jot down "surprises" — things that feel off, puzzling observations, irritations, fascinations
- Topics emerge from what bothers him, not what he decides to write about
- Mull ideas over for a few days before writing

### Phase 2: Question Framing
- Frame as a genuine question, not a thesis
- "Why does so much school writing feel dead?" not "School writing is dead because..."
- Preserves honesty when the draft veers in unexpected directions
- Don't try to think of ideas — "the way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas"

### Phase 3: Fast, Messy First Draft
- "Write a bad version 1 as fast as you can"
- Zero expectation of coherence or persuasion
- Purpose: get half-formed thoughts into the open
- Follow interest from paragraph to paragraph — no outline
- Let the essay meander; dead ends mean backing up and trying a different branch
- "If you can't get started, tell someone what you plan to write about, then write down what you said"

### Phase 4: Extraction
- Hunt for what's worth keeping
- Three tests: what surprised him, what a smart reader would care about, what only he could write
- Filter through the Morris test: did I learn something?

### Phase 5: Intensive Rewriting (The Core)
- "Rewrite it over and over"
- Go over every sentence 20–50 times, some 100+ times
- Read as "a skeptical, slightly impatient stranger"
- Two signals: boredom (attention drifts) and friction (claims don't convince)
- "If a sentence sounds wrong, it usually means the thought underneath is still half-baked" — fix the IDEA, not just the wording
- Read aloud to catch awkward phrases AND boring passages
- Cut anything boring, rethink anything fuzzy, simplify any sentence embarrassing to say aloud

### Phase 6: External Feedback
- Give draft to "one or two smart friends"
- Specific questions: where did you get bored? where did you get lost? which sentence will I regret most?
- When two readers flag the same spot, it's a real problem
- Use readers "not to rubber-stamp ideas but to locate blind spots"

### Phase 7: Final Shaping
- Trim wandering path into something direct while preserving feel of live thinking
- Move digressions to footnotes
- Watch for repetition, overly sharp lines he might regret, side-ideas that deserve their own essays
- Go back and "tone down harsh remarks"
- Print out drafts — "don't just look at them on the screen"
- "Learn to recognize the approach of an ending, and when one appears, grab it"

### Phase 8: Publication as Continuation
- Posting is "throwing the idea into the world to see what questions, objections, and misunderstandings come back"
- Reader reactions generate new surprises for the notebook
- "An audience makes you write more, and thus generate more ideas"

## 5. Sentence-Level Patterns

### Opening Sentences
- Often a short, arresting claim or question
- "When we were in junior high school, my friend Rich and I made a map" (anecdote)
- "I think it's far more important to write well than most people realize" (bold claim)
- "In the process of answering an email, I accidentally wrote a tiny essay about writing" (meta)

### Paragraph Structure
- Topic sentence → 1-2 supporting sentences → transition or punch
- Very short — 2 to 4 sentences typical
- Each paragraph is visually lightweight, encouraging reading

### Sentence Variety
- Mix of very short ("But in fact I didn't, not enough") and longer explanatory sentences
- Paratactic (coordinate) over hypotactic (subordinate) structure
- Frequent dashes for asides
- Occasional semicolons for closely related clauses
- Parenthetical digressions create conversational intimacy

### Rhetorical Devices
- **Anaphora**: intentional repetition of sentence starts to knit ideas together
- **Epistrophe**: occasional repetition at sentence ends for emphasis
- **Chiasmus**: rare but effective ("half technology and half religion")
- **Metaphor**: the dominant cognitive tool. Vivid, concrete, often extended
- **Direct address**: "you" is the default pronoun
- **Imperatives**: "Don't let..." "Write..." "Cut..." — commanding but earned
- **Hypophora**: posing a question then answering it
- **Enumeration**: numbered lists within prose, bold headers as structural signposts

### Punctuation Patterns
- Em-dashes (—) for asides and emphasis — very frequent
- Parentheses for quieter asides (less frequent than dashes)
- Periods are preferred; sentences end definitively
- Semicolons used sparingly but precisely
- Exclamation marks: essentially never
- Question marks: for genuine questions, not rhetorical padding

## 6. What Makes PG Writing Instantly Recognizable

If you read a paragraph blind, here's what signals "this is Paul Graham":

1. **No hedging anywhere** — claims are stated as facts
2. **"You" addressing the reader directly** — feels like a conversation
3. **A vivid analogy** — something concrete compared to something abstract
4. **Short, punchy sentences** — no sprawling subordinate clauses
5. **A contrarian assertion** — challenging conventional wisdom
6. **An anecdote from his own life** — YC, Viaweb, painting, parenting
7. **Bold text headers** — structural signposts
8. **A footnote adding nuance** — the main text stays clean
9. **Simple words for complex ideas** — "weird" not "anomalous"
10. **An aphoristic closer** — a compressed restatement that sticks
